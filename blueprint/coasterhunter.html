01 · The name

CoasterHunter — decided, and the .com is free

This is now the name. A live registry check on every candidate below came back clean for it — and it was the strongest of the set anyway, because "hunter" is what enthusiasts already do: they hunt credits. It carries the collecting instinct without leaning on fitness language, and it gives you a verb ("hunting", "on the hunt") and a noun for the community ("Hunters") for free.

02 · The concept

Three layers, stacked in the order people adopt them

LogRide has one layer and does it very well. That's also its ceiling: it's a database you type into. CoasterHunter ships that layer at parity on day one so nobody has to give anything up to switch — then earns its subscription on the two layers above it.

Layer 1 — table stakes

The Log

The world's parks, coasters, flats, dark rides, water rides, shows, haunts and travelling midways. Credits, tallies, check-ins, trip reports, defunct attractions. Full LogRide parity, plus a proper import so a LogRide user's history comes across intact.

Layer 2 — the moat

The Sensor

The watch samples accelerometer and gyro at 100 Hz through the ride. It recognises which coaster you're on, then hands back peak G, sustained negative G, airtime seconds, inversion count, lateral load and a roughness index — as a signature trace unique to that lap, in that row, on that train.

Layer 3 — retention

The Game

XP, levels, streaks, badge sets, seasonal challenges and leaderboards that reset — global, national, per-park, per-ride, friends-only. Plus pairwise ranking that builds a genuinely defensible personal Top 100 instead of another five-star average.

Why the order matters. The sensor layer is the reason to download and the reason to talk about it. But it can't be the reason to open the app on a Tuesday in February — so the log and the game carry the off-season, and the sensor carries the summer.

04 · Appearance

Light, dark, or whatever the phone says

Three states, not two — and the third is the default. Most people never open the setting, so the un-set state has to follow the system perfectly. Both palettes are designed, not inverted: the dark ground is a slightly blue near-black so the mesh gradients don't go muddy against it, and the light ground is a warm-neutral grey rather than white so white cards still read as cards.

The one rule that breaks apps. Semantic colours that sit on a coloured surface (green on a mesh tile) can stay saturated; the same colour as text on a plain ground cannot. Airtime green needs its own darker value in light mode or it fails contrast on white. Two tokens, one concept — that's why --air and --air-ink are separate above.

06 · What to add

Twenty-two ideas, five of which are the product

Outlined cards are the ones I'd protect in scope negotiations. They're the reasons someone chooses this over a free database with a $0.99 upgrade.

SENSOR — SIGNATURE

RideDNA

Every lap produces a trace: vertical G, lateral G and rotation over time, with airtime bands shaded and inversions marked. No two laps are identical, which is exactly the point — it makes a ride log into an artefact worth sharing, and gives you a poster to print and sell.

NewShareableMerch line
SENSOR — DEFENSIBILITY

Roughness index

Jerk — the rate of change of G — averaged across every lap the community logs, per ride. Enthusiasts argue about which Vekoma rattles worst and which RMC has aged badly. You'd be the only source of an actual number, and it becomes the stat the forums quote. That's how the app gets talked about for free.

NewCrowd data
GAME — RANKING

Pairwise Top 100

Never ask for a score out of ten. Ask "Nemesis or Icon?" and run Elo. Twenty comparisons produce a better-ordered personal Top 100 than a hundred star ratings, it's addictive in queues, and aggregated it yields a community Top 100 that is statistically defensible — which is a publishable, press-friendly asset every single year.

NewRetention loopPR asset
SENSOR — DATA MOAT

True wait times

You already know when someone joined a queue and when their ride started, because the watch told you. That's a measured wait, not a posted one. Show both, and the gap between them, for every ride in every park — including the thousands of parks with no official feed. LogRide structurally cannot do this.

Park plan

Give it a park, your arrival and leaving times, and your un-ridden list; it returns a walking order that front-loads the rides that build the longest queues and slots the rest by proximity. Re-plans live as waits move. This is the feature that converts a free user on the morning of a trip.

Log which row you sat in and the app shows how the profile changes front to back. Settles the oldest argument in the hobby with data from your own laps.

08 · The hard part

How ride detection actually works — and where it breaks

This is the feature the whole product rests on, so it deserves an honest description rather than a promise. It is very achievable. It is not free.

SIGNAL

Sample only inside a park

A geofence around the park turns on a low-rate accelerometer watch. Below about 0.3 G of sustained deviation nothing happens, so the battery cost of walking around is negligible. A launch spike or a sustained lift-hill signature promotes to 100 Hz capture on accelerometer, gyroscope and barometer.

MATCH

Fingerprint against a template library

Each ride has a template built from community laps. Matching a live trace to it is dynamic time warping over the normalised vertical-G envelope — robust to different train speeds and slightly different ride times. Combined with GPS position at the station, a confident match is straightforward; the ambiguity is between near-identical clone models in the same park, which is exactly what the location fix resolves.

COLD START

The first laps have to be manual

There is no template library on day one. Launch with confirm-or-correct — "Was that Nemesis?" — and every confirmation adds a lap to the template. Seed the top 500 coasters yourself before launch by riding them or recruiting a beta group; that covers the large majority of logging volume.

Budget for this. It is a real, physical, several-week job.
DERIVE

Metrics from the trace

Airtime is time below 0.5 G on the vertical axis; sustained airtime is any continuous run over 0.4 s. Inversions come from integrating gyroscope roll and pitch past 180°. Roughness is RMS jerk in the 4–20 Hz band, which is where rattle lives and where arm movement mostly isn't. Peak G is trivially the max, but must be filtered for the moment someone raises their arms.

INTEGRITY

Assume people will cheat the leaderboard

Any leaderboard with a number on it will be gamed by someone shaking a watch on a sofa. Three checks handle nearly all of it: the trace must match a known template above threshold, the device must be inside the park geofence, and lap timing must be physically plausible against the ride's cycle time and the day's throughput. Flag rather than ban, and review.

REALITY

What you can't control

Some parks require loose articles including watches to go in a locker — a growing list, and there is no software answer. Manual logging must stay a first-class path, never a fallback that feels like failure. Water rides will drown a non-rated watch. And Wear OS sensor quality varies enormously across manufacturers, so Android needs a per-device calibration table and a graceful "estimated" state.

Say this in the App Store listing. Overpromising here generates one-star reviews you can't answer.
09 · Build

Native where it counts, shared where it doesn't

Decided

iOS first — SwiftUI + watchOS

Android is deferred. The enthusiast community skews Apple, Apple Watch sensor data is far more consistent than Wear OS across manufacturers, and LogRide is iOS-only — so Android is a second market rather than a requirement. Everything that isn't a view lives in a platform-agnostic Swift package: the g-force maths, the trace matcher, the ranking engine and the pass clock. That package is unit-tested and stays portable if Android is funded later.

Built so far

Where the project actually is

The data pipeline is working and tested, and produces a 7.1 MB database covering 198 parks and 8,229 attractions. The core Swift package — sensor maths, trace matching, Elo ranking, entitlements — passes 61 tests. The SwiftUI and watchOS layers are written and typechecked but not yet compiled for iOS, because Xcode isn't installed on the build machine yet.

10 · Sequence

What to build, in what order

Ordered by dependency, not by excitement. Each phase is shippable on its own.

01

Database & the log

Seed database built and tested · app data layer next

Parks, attractions, spec sheets, submissions pipeline, moderation tooling, accounts, check-in, tallies, trip reports, LogRide import. No sensors. This phase is where the project succeeds or dies, and it is entirely a data-operations problem wearing an engineering costume.

02

Watch logging & the sensor pipeline

Months 4–7 · beta with 50–100 enthusiasts

watchOS app, capture, metric derivation, RideDNA rendering, confirm-or-correct detection. Ship to a closed beta and use their laps to build the template library for the top 500 coasters.

03

Public launch, free tier only

Month 8 · deliberately before monetising

Free everything for one season. You need laps in the template library and users on leaderboards far more than you need the first £2.99. Launch at the start of the season, not the end of it.

04

Pro, seasons, pairwise ranking

Months 9–12 · monetisation on

Paywall, Pro features, first season with resetting leaderboards, Top 100 engine, first Wrapped in December — which is the single best organic acquisition moment of the year, so do not miss it.

05

Planning, Android, events

Year 2

Park Plan and the road trip builder, true wait times once density allows, Android and Wear OS, the Crew and Events tier, and the first published community Top 100 as a press moment.

11 · Open items

What stands between here and a running app

Blocker

Xcode and ~30 GB of disk

Nothing can be compiled for iOS or seen in the Simulator until Xcode 15.2 is installed. macOS 13 Ventura caps the toolchain there, which is why the deployment target is iOS 17 — no macOS upgrade required.

Needed to ship

Apple Developer Program

£79/yr, required before the app can go on your watch or into TestFlight. Not needed to keep building — StoreKit is testable locally against a configuration file.

Still urgent

Buy the domains this week

coasterhunter.com, .co.uk, .io and .co are all still unregistered, plus the social handles. Availability today is not availability next month, and name checks like this leave traces.

The real test

Record laps on a ride you know

The metric thresholds are reasoned, not calibrated — there are no real traces yet. Once the app is on your watch, a handful of laps on rides with published figures is what proves or disproves the whole premise. Worth doing early.
